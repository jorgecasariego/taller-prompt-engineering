2. Anatomy of a Prompt
Overview
Most effective prompts, even when they look like a single block of text, are built from a small set of recurring parts. Learning to see those parts gives you a checklist for writing prompts and a way to debug them when the output disappoints. The four parts that matter most are the instruction, the context, the input, and the output format. Not every prompt needs all four, but knowing they exist helps you notice what is missing.
The instruction is the core command: what you want the model to do. The context is the supporting information the model needs to do it well, such as background, constraints, or audience. The input is the specific material the instruction operates on, for example the text to summarize or the data to classify. The output format describes the shape of the response you expect, such as a bulleted list, a table, valid JSON, or a paragraph of a certain length.
Separating these parts has two benefits. First, it forces you to supply each one consciously instead of cramming everything into a single hurried sentence. Second, it makes prompts easier to revise, because you can adjust one part, such as the output format, without rewriting the whole thing. As prompts grow longer, clear separation also helps the model tell your instructions apart from the data it should process.
In practice, you will often label these parts explicitly with headings or delimiters, especially when the input is long or could be confused with your instructions. This structure is one of the simplest, highest-impact habits in prompt engineering.
Key concepts
The instruction should be specific and action-oriented. Prefer concrete verbs and measurable targets. "Summarize the text below in three bullet points" is clearer than "tell me about this." When a task has several requirements, list them so none gets lost.
The context supplies what the model cannot infer: the audience, the purpose, relevant facts, the tone, or rules it must follow. Context is also where you place constraints, such as "do not invent statistics" or "only use information from the provided text." Good context reduces the model's need to guess.
The input is the actual data. When the input is more than a sentence or two, wrap it in clear delimiters such as triple quotes, triple backticks, or XML-style tags, and refer to it by name. This prevents the model from mistaking part of your input for an instruction, a common cause of strange output.
The output format tells the model the exact structure you need. If you want machine-readable output, specify the format precisely and give a small schema or example. If you want prose, state the length and style. Being explicit here is often the difference between an answer you can use directly and one you must reformat by hand.
Order matters too. A reliable pattern is: instruction first, then context, then the clearly delimited input, then a restatement of the output format. Putting the format near the end keeps it fresh as the model begins generating.
